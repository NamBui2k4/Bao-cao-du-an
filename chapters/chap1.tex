\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background and Rationale}

The global education landscape has undergone a profound paradigm shift, accelerated by the increasing demand for remote learning and digital literacy. Learning Management Systems (LMS) have transitioned from being supplementary tools to becoming the core infrastructure of modern academic institutions. As user bases grow from hundreds to millions of concurrent learners, the underlying software architecture of these platforms faces unprecedented pressure. The architectural integrity of an LMS is no longer just a technical detail; it is a strategic foundation that determines the system's ability to remain available under seasonal traffic spikes, such as during national examinations or enrollment periods.

Traditionally, software development favored the \textit{Monolithic} approach due to its simplicity in initial deployment and cohesive data management. However, as systems evolve in complexity, the rigid coupling inherent in Monoliths often leads to "architectural erosion," where minor changes in one module can trigger unpredictable failures across the entire system. In response, the \textit{Microservice} architecture has emerged as a disruptive alternative, promising independent scalability and fault isolation by decomposing the system into smaller, loosely coupled services. 

Despite the widespread adoption of Microservice by industry giants, it introduces a "complexity tax," involving distributed networking overhead and data consistency challenges. This research is motivated by the need to provide a rigorous, empirical comparison between these dominant paradigms within the specific functional requirements of an LMS—covering identity management, course delivery, and high-stakes assessment. By quantifying the trade-offs in performance, resilience, and resource efficiency, this study aims to provide a decision-making blueprint for educational technology architects.

\section{Project Objectives}

The overarching goal of this research is to architect, implement, and rigorously evaluate a Learning Management System (LMS) across contrasting architectural paradigms. The study aims to move beyond theoretical comparisons by providing an empirical data-driven analysis of how Monolithic and Microservice models behave under identical operational pressures, ultimately determining their suitability for various organizational scales and technical requirements.

To achieve this, the project is structured around five core research objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{High-Fidelity System Design and Domain Specification:}
The first objective focuses on designing a comprehensive LMS framework that serves as a stable "functional baseline." This involves:
\begin{itemize}
\item Developing a detailed Software Requirements Specification (SRS) for core academic workflows, including Identity and Access Management (IAM), Course Lifecycle Management, Content Delivery, and complex Assessment engines.
\item Utilizing Domain-Driven Design (DDD) to identify "Bounded Contexts," ensuring that the business logic is modular enough to be implemented as either internal packages (Monolith) or independent services (Microservice) without altering the core functionality.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Dual-Architectural Implementation and Environment Parity:}
The project aims to develop two distinct, production-ready versions of the LMS. The focus is to maintain a \textit{ceteris paribus} (all other things being equal) condition:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Implementing the Monolithic version as a single-process application using NestJS, ensuring centralized data management and low-latency internal communication.
    \item Implementing the Microservice version using a "Database-per-Service" pattern, coordinated via an API Gateway and asynchronous message brokering (RabbitMQ).
    \item Utilizing Docker and Docker Compose to ensure both architectures operate within identical infrastructure constraints, eliminating environment-induced performance variances.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Empirical Performance Benchmarking and Stress Testing:}
To quantify the "Performance Tax" of each architecture, the study will conduct systematic load tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Measuring raw Throughput (Requests Per Second) and Tail Latency (P95/P99) under diverse load profiles, including "Step Loads" for scalability and "Flash Crowds" for peak stress resilience.
    \item Calculating the Resource Utilization Efficiency (RUE) to determine the marginal CPU and RAM consumption per active learner, providing a clear picture of the infrastructure costs associated with each model.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Simulation of Software Evolution and Maintainability:}
This objective evaluates how each architecture responds to the inevitable changes in a software lifecycle. The study will model "Controlled Change Scenarios" to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Measure the development effort and "Ripple Effect" (regression risks) when modifying core business logic, such as grading algorithms.
    \item Analyze the complexity of "Schema Evolution" and feature extensions (e.g., adding a learning analytics module) to observe the trade-offs between shared and isolated data stores.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Comparative Synthesis and Decision-Support Framework:}
The final objective is to synthesize all quantitative and qualitative data into a coherent evaluation matrix. This involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Analyzing the balance between "System Complexity" and "Performance Gains" to identify the "Break-even Point" where the overhead of Microservice becomes a justified investment.
    \item Providing a strategic recommendation model for educational institutions, helping them choose an architecture based on their specific constraints in terms of team size, traffic variability, and long-term maintainability.
\end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Project}

The primary focus of this project is a comparative analysis of software architectural paradigms; the LMS platform serves as a standardized experimental vehicle rather than a commercial end-product. To ensure the scientific validity of the comparison, the scope is strictly defined within the following boundaries:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Functional Stability and Code Parity:}
Across all architectural implementations, the core business logic—encompassing Identity Management, Enrollment Workflows, Course Content Delivery, and Assessment Engines—remains functionally identical. A "Code Parity" policy is enforced: any algorithmic optimization made in the Monolithic version is mirrored in the Microservice version. This ensures that observed performance variances are strictly a result of architectural topology (e.g., network hops, serialization) rather than disparities in code efficiency.

\item \textbf{Data Architecture and Persistence Scope:} 
The study covers two distinct data management strategies. For the Monolithic model, the scope includes a centralized relational schema within a single PostgreSQL instance. For the Microservice model, the scope shifts to a "Database-per-Service" pattern, utilizing isolated schemas to evaluate the impact of data decentralization on system consistency and complexity. The research limits its focus to relational data; NoSQL or Graph databases are excluded to maintain a controlled comparison environment.

\item \textbf{Technological and Infrastructure Constraints:} 
The research utilizes a unified, industry-standard technology stack to eliminate "language-induced" bias.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Backend:} NestJS (Node.js) is chosen for its modular architecture, allowing for a seamless transition between monolithic modules and Microservice.
    \item \textbf{Communication:} The scope includes synchronous RESTful APIs for the Monolith and a hybrid of REST and asynchronous Message Brokering (via RabbitMQ) for Microservice.
    \item \textbf{Deployment:} Both systems are containerized using \textit{Docker} and orchestrated via \textit{Docker Compose}. The evaluation is conducted within a controlled virtual network to ensure "Environmental Parity," where CPU and RAM limits are strictly enforced at the container level.
\end{itemize}

\item \textbf{Evaluation Metrics and Analytical Boundaries:} 
The project limits its quantitative analysis to specific Key Performance Indicators (KPIs): Throughput (Requests Per Second), Tail Latency (P95/P99), and Resource Utilization Efficiency (RUE). Qualitative analysis is bounded by "Controlled Change Scenarios" designed to simulate real-world maintenance tasks. 

\item \textbf{Exclusions and Out-of-Scope Elements:}
To maintain a focused research trajectory, the following elements are explicitly excluded:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Front-end Complexity:} The User Interface (UI) is developed as a "Minimum Viable Product" (MVP) using Vue.js, prioritizing functionality for data verification over aesthetic design or advanced User Experience (UX) features.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Architectures:} Alternative models such as Serverless, fully decentralized Event-Driven Architectures (EDA), or Service Meshes (e.g., Istio) are reserved for future work and are outside the current comparative framework.
    \item \textbf{Security Auditing:} While standard JWT-based authentication is implemented, deep penetration testing and exhaustive security audits are not within the scope of this architectural study.
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Methodology}

This study adopts a comparative and empirical research design, emphasizing a quantitative and qualitative architectural analysis. The methodology is structured to isolate architectural variables, ensuring that performance and operational differences observed between the \textit{Monolithic} and \textit{Microservice} implementations are attributable solely to their structural paradigms.

\subsection{Theoretical Framework and Comparison Basis}

The foundational phase involves a systematic review of architectural design patterns. By synthesizing contemporary theories~\cite{IJNRD2023}, a standardized evaluation framework is established, focusing on three analytical pillars:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Performance and Scalability Efficiency:} Evaluating throughput and response-time degradation curves under elastic workloads.
    
    \item \textbf{Fault Tolerance and Resilience Topology:} Investigating the "Blast Radius" of localized failures and the system's ability to maintain partial availability.
    
    \item \textbf{Resource Optimization:} Measuring the marginal resource consumption (CPU/RAM) required per unit of throughput in a cloud-native context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Experimental Design and Implementation}

The core research involves implementing the LMS twice, following different architectural blueprints but maintaining strict business logic parity.

\subsubsection{Functional Domain Decomposition}
Using a Software Requirements Specification (SRS), the LMS is decomposed into \textit{Bounded Contexts}. This ensures that domains like \textit{Assessment} and \textit{Course Management} are consistent across versions, eliminating software-level bias and maintaining a \textit{ceteris paribus} condition.

\subsubsection{Environment Control}
Both architectures are orchestrated via \textit{Docker Compose}. This approach guarantees environment parity, ensuring that networking latency and runtime overhead are consistent across all test cycles.

\subsection{Quantitative Evaluation Metrics}

The study employs scenario-based stress testing to extract granular data:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Performance (Throughput \& Latency):} Measuring Requests Per Second (RPS) and P95/P99 "Tail Latency" to identify system bottlenecks.
    
    \item \textbf{Resource Utilization Efficiency (RUE):} Calculating the Resource Utilization Index to measure the CPU/RAM processing capacity consumed relative to workload intensity.
    
    \item \textbf{Fault Tolerance Assessment:} Measuring \textit{Mean Time To Recovery} (MTTR) and the \textit{Service Availability Index} after injecting failures into non-critical modules.
    
    \item \textbf{Total Cost of Ownership (TCO):} Analyzing infrastructure footprint and operational complexity involved in deployment and service orchestration.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Maintainability and Evolution Scenarios}

Three "Change Scenarios" are introduced to simulate long-term maintenance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logic Modification:} Assessing regression risks during algorithm updates.
    \item \textbf{Feature Extension:} Measuring the effort to integrate learning analytics.
    \item \textbf{Schema Evolution:} Evaluating the impact of data structure changes on interconnected modules (Shared DB vs. Isolated DB).
\end{itemize}
