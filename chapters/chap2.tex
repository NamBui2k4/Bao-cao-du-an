\chapter{Literature Review}

This chapter provides a comprehensive synthesis of the theoretical foundations and empirical evidence governing the transition from Monolithic to Microservice architectures, specifically within the domain of Learning Management Systems (LMS). By critically evaluating established principles such as the CAP theorem, the "Infrastructure Tax" phenomenon, and modularity patterns, this review identifies a significant research gap: the lack of multi-dimensional, LMS-specific benchmarks that integrate performance, resource efficiency, and development-phase resilience. The following sections establish the conceptual vocabulary and methodological precedents—ranging from host-level CPU monitoring to availability metrics—that form the baseline for this study’s comparative framework. Ultimately, this review moves beyond the binary "Monolith versus Microservice" debate to explore how architectural topology dictates system behavior under the heterogeneous read/write workloads characteristic of modern educational environments.

\section{Related Works}

This section reviews the existing body of knowledge regarding architectural evolution, performance trade-offs, and empirical comparisons between Monolithic and Microservice paradigms. By synthesizing findings from recent studies and industry reports, this review establishes the theoretical framework and identifies the research gaps that this project aims to address through its comparative LMS implementation.

\subsection{Architectural Evolution in E-Learning Systems}

Learning Management Systems have undergone substantial architectural evolution over the past two decades. 
Early platforms such as Moodle, Blackboard, and Canvas were designed as tightly-coupled monolithic applications 
to simplify deployment in institutional settings where a single server and unified codebase minimised operational 
overhead \cite{Pia_Niemela}. Their architecture reflected the prevailing assumption that a university's 
user population would remain bounded and predictable — an assumption invalidated by the rapid growth of Massive 
Open Online Courses (MOOCs) and, more recently, by the COVID-19 pandemic's surge in remote education, which 
temporarily multiplied concurrent user loads by factors of three to five at many institutions \cite{IJACSA2023}.

As LMS deployments scaled to tens of thousands of concurrent learners, the structural limitations of 
shared-state, single-process architectures became apparent in at least three ways. First, database 
connection pools shared across all modules (authentication, course content, quiz grading) created 
contention hotspots: a spike in quiz submissions degraded course-browsing response times for unrelated 
users. Second, scaling the entire monolith to address a bottleneck in one module (e.g., video streaming) 
was operationally wasteful and financially costly, as it replicated underutilised capacity. Third, the 
tight coupling of modules within a shared codebase meant that even minor changes — such as updating a 
grading rubric — required full system rebuilds and re-deployments, extending Mean Time To Recovery (MTTR) 
and reducing development velocity.

Niemelä and Hyyrö (2019) were among the first to formally propose a migration pathway from a monolithic 
LMS to a Microservice architecture, arguing that service decomposition along pedagogical boundaries 
(content delivery, assessment, analytics) aligns naturally with the modular structure of course design 
and enables independent scaling of high-demand subsystems \cite{Pia_Niemela}. Their framework, 
however, remained theoretical and lacked empirical performance data. Dinh (2023) subsequently documented 
the technical debt accumulation patterns in a production monolithic LMS deployed at a Vietnamese 
university, reporting that over 60\% of system downtime incidents were attributable to database 
lock contention caused by the absence of service-level isolation, and proposing a phased 
decomposition strategy \cite{nguyen_van_dinh_2023}. Hasan et al. (2023) extended this line of work by measuring 
the maintainability impact of architectural migration using code-level metrics (cyclomatic complexity, 
afferent and efferent coupling), finding that a Microservice refactoring of a higher-education 
information system reduced cross-module dependencies by up to 43\% and cut the average number of 
files touched per feature change by approximately 60\% \cite{IJACSA2023}.

\subsection{The CAP Theorem and Architectural Trade-offs}
In the context of distributed E-learning systems, the CAP theorem (Consistency, Availability, and Partition Tolerance) serves as a foundational theoretical constraint. A Monolithic LMS inherently prioritizes \textbf{Consistency (C)} through its unified database and ACID transactions, ensuring that a student's grade or enrollment status is immediately reflected system-wide. However, this often comes at the expense of \textbf{Availability (A)} during high-traffic surges, where a single point of failure can paralyze the entire application.

Conversely, the transition to Microservice necessitates a shift toward \textbf{Availability} and \textbf{Partition Tolerance (P)}. By adopting an "Eventual Consistency" model, Microservice allow individual modules (e.g., Course Content) to remain accessible even if the Grading service is under heavy load or temporarily unavailable. This research examines how this theoretical shift impacts the user experience during peak academic periods, balancing the need for immediate data accuracy against the necessity of continuous system uptime.

\subsection{Empirical Performance Comparisons} 

A substantial body of empirical literature has attempted quantitative performance comparisons between Monolithic and 
Microservice architectures, though the results are highly context-dependent. Al-Debagy and Martinek (2018) 
performed controlled load tests on functionally equivalent 
RESTful applications, reporting that throughput differences between the two architectures 
averaged only 0.87\% under gradually increasing load, but diverged to approximately 6\% 
under simultaneous concurrent request injection, with the Monolith retaining a throughput 
advantage at that specific scenario because the absence of inter-service network hops allows it to process 
requests with lower per-request overhead \cite{Omar_Al-Debagy}. Their study established 
the methodological baseline adopted by subsequent researchers: using the same business-domain 
endpoints, identical hardware, and two distinct load scenarios (gradual ramp-up and concurrent surge).

Schlögl et al. (2024) refined this methodology by decomposing resource consumption measurement 
into three distinct test cases based on operation complexity: simple database 
query (analogous to GET /courses), intensive computation (data transformation), and distributed 
multi-service orchestration (analogous to enrolment workflows spanning authentication, 
course validation, and progress tracking) \cite{Schloegl2024}. Their key contribution 
was the observation that measuring CPU only at the application-service layer systematically 
underestimates Microservice overhead: only by aggregating host-level container 
metrics (equivalent to host\_total in the current study) does the full cost of API Gateway 
routing, service discovery, and health-check polling become visible. This methodological insight 
directly informed the measurement design of the present study, which uses host\_total as the 
primary CPU comparison metric for Microservice.

Tapia et al. (2020) conducted a systematic review of 31 primary studies comparing monolithic 
and Microservice performance, finding that reported results were highly sensitive to the 
application domain, hardware environment, and load pattern used [Tapia et al., 2020]. Particularly 
relevant to the present work is their finding that e-commerce and financial systems — the domains 
most commonly studied — exhibit workload patterns (uniform transaction types, moderate concurrency) 
that differ substantially from the uneven read/write distributions characteristic of LMS deployments.
This contextual gap is one of the primary motivations for the present study. Faye and Sheikh (2025) 
complemented these performance findings with a cost analysis across 23 architectural migration cases, 
reporting an average infrastructure cost increase of 47\% and a DevOps tooling cost increase of 150–275\% 
when transitioning to Microservice — findings that contextualise the Resource Usage Efficiency (RUE) 
metric adopted in this project's cost analysis \cite{Yogant_Faye}.

\subsection{Architectural Patterns}
Recent literature has begun questioning the binary framing of 'Monolith versus Microservice', proposing 
instead a spectrum of architectural options. Dragoni et al. (2017) provided a comprehensive theoretical 
analysis of the Microservice paradigm, identifying accumulated serialisation/deserialisation costs and 
connection pool management as the primary mechanisms through which inter-service latency accumulates 
under high concurrency \cite{Nicola_Dragoni}. Taibi et al. (2018) mapped the most commonly adopted 
Microservice architectural patterns across 60 industrial case studies, finding that the API Gateway 
(adopted in 87\% of cases) and the Service Mesh pattern (adopted in 41\%) are the dominant approaches 
to managing inter-service communication overhead [Taibi et al., 2018]. Xu (2025) synthesised these 
findings into a practitioner-oriented decision framework, concluding that for teams with fewer than 
10 developers or systems with fewer than 1,000 daily active users, the operational overhead of 
Microservice rarely delivers proportional resilience or scalability benefits — a finding particularly 
relevant to small and mid-sized LMS deployments \cite{Alex_xu_2025}.

Three research gaps are evident from this synthesis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gap 1 — LMS-specific context. No prior empirical study benchmarks both architectures in an LMS environment with the read/write workload heterogeneity characteristic of real learning workflows (course browsing, enrolment, assessment submission). \section{Theoretical Principles and Architectural Models}

This section establishes the theoretical foundation for the comparative analysis by defining the core characteristics, advantages, and inherent constraints of the two architectural paradigms under investigation. By exploring the principles of modularity, data sovereignty, and communication overhead, this chapter provides the conceptual lens through which the empirical data—ranging from throughput crossover points to resource efficiency—will be interpreted. The goal is to move beyond a simple "monolith versus Microservice" dichotomy and instead examine how structural design choices dictate the operational limits of a Learning Management System.

The comparative framework applied in this study is grounded in established theoretical principles from 
software architecture research. This section synthesizes the relevant principles and defines the conceptual 
vocabulary used in the experimental evaluation.

\subsection{Monolithic Architecture}
The Monolithic implementation packages all LMS functionality as a single NestJS application sharing a common PostgreSQL 
database schema. Internal communication occurs through direct function calls within the same process, 
eliminating inter-service network latency. Deployment consists of a single Docker container.
This model offers simplicity for initial development but creates tight coupling at the database and 
code levels. For example, changes to grading logic or progress tracking  
require will rebuild and redeploy the entire application. Scaling is only possible at the 
application level—addressing CPU bottlenecks in quiz submission processing requires scaling 
the entire monolith, even if other modules remain lightly loaded.

\end{figure}

\subsection{Microservice Architecture}
The Microservice implementation decomposes the LMS into many independent services 
communicating via REST APIs and message broker. Each service maintains its own 
database schema and can be deployed or scaled independently. This decomposition brings trade-offs. 
For instance, independent deployability allows changes to affect only  Grading Service. However, 
network communication between services introduces latency and failure modes. Cross-service operations (e.g., enrollment validation requires 
Academic Service to verify course status via Course Service API) require explicit coordination. 
Operational complexity increases—service discovery, distributed tracing, and health monitoring 
are required infrastructure absent in Monolithic deployments. 

For LMS deployments serving fewer than 500 concurrent users — the operational scale at which the Monolith's throughput advantage persists, as established by the crossover analysis in Section 6.1 — the additional infrastructure complexity of Microservices must be justified by measurable fault isolation or long-term scalability requirements that outweigh the coordination overhead cost.

\section{Comparison Methodology}

This section delineates the multi-dimensional methodology employed to evaluate the trade-offs between Monolithic and Microservice architectures within the context of a Learning Management System (LMS). By synthesizing empirical frameworks from established literature, the evaluation is structured around four critical pillars: performance dynamics (throughput and response time), resource efficiency (CPU utilization), system resilience (fault tolerance), and economic viability (cost efficiency). The methodology transitions from black-box load testing to white-box resource monitoring, ensuring that the "Architectural Tax" of distributed systems is quantified against the operational agility they provide. This comprehensive benchmarking approach serves to validate theoretical claims with empirical data, providing a rigorous baseline for the subsequent analysis of architectural suitability across varying operational scales.

\subsection{Throughput \& Response time}

Performance constitutes a critical evaluation criterion when comparing software architectures, particularly 
in educational systems where concurrent user access during peak periods (enrollment, exam submissions, 
assignment deadlines) directly impacts user experience and system reliability. The empirical 
methodology established by Al-Debagy and Martinek \cite{Omar_Al-Debagy} performed three
scenarios to evaluate through two primary metrics: \textbf{throughput} (requests handled per second) and 
\textbf{response time} (latency between request and response).


The findings of Al-Debagy and Martinek suggest that the architectural performance gap is a 
function of 'Communication Friction.' In a Monolith, service-to-service calls are handled via 
in-memory method invocations, whereas Microservice require a complex stack of network 
protocols, data serialization (e.g., JSON/Protobuf), and service resolution, all of 
which contribute to the 'Performance Tax' observed in high-concurrency environments.

\subsubsection{First scenario: Load Testing} 

The study simulates realistic growth patterns by gradually 
increasing concurrent users from 100 to 7,000 threads with a 2-minute ramp-up period and 2-minute 
hold time at each level. This approach mirrors typical LMS usage patterns where user load increases 
progressively during registration periods or as students access course materials throughout the semester.

It points out that both architectures exhibit comparable throughput 
performance under normal operating conditions, with differences averaging only 0.87\%. However, the 
monolithic architecture shows a slight advantage at lower concurrency levels (100-1,000 threads), 
which the authors attribute to the overhead of inter-service communication in Microservice architecture. 
As load increases to 6,000-7,000 threads, performance converges, suggesting that Microservice' 
distributed nature may offset communication overhead under high load.

Response time analysis (Figure \ref{fig:load-response}) reveals no significant architectural difference, 
with both monolithic and Microservice implementations exhibiting comparable latency degradation

\subsubsection{Second scenario: Concurrency Testing}

To evaluate architecture performance under peak stress conditions — such as when hundreds of students 
simultaneously submit assignments before a deadline — Al-Debagy and Martinek conducted concurrency 
tests where all service requests are invoked simultaneously without ramp-up periods. This scenario 
represents worst-case load conditions for LMS deployments.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/papers/concurency-throughput.png}
    \caption{Throughput under simultaneous request load.}
    \label{fig:concurrency-throughput}
\end{figure}

Under concurrent load conditions (Figure \ref{fig:concurrency-throughput}), the performance gap 
between architectures becomes more pronounced. The monolithic architecture exhibits approximately 
6\% higher throughput on average, processing requests more efficiently when all services are invoked 
simultaneously. This advantage stems from reduced inter-service communication latency and the absence 
of network overhead between components — factors that become critical bottlenecks when Microservice 
must coordinate responses to sudden traffic spikes.

This 6\% throughput advantage of the Monolith is further explained by the Serialization/Deserialization overhead. 
In the Microservice version of an LMS, a single student request (e.g., viewing a full course profile) 
may trigger multiple internal calls between the \textit{Course Service}, \textit{Material Service}, 
and \textit{Account Service}. Each hop requires the system to transform data into a transmittable format, 
increasing CPU cycles per request. Under peak stress, this 'Computation Inflation' limits the total number 
of requests the Microservice gateway can fulfill compared to the Monolith, which accesses shared memory pointers directly.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/papers/concurency-response.png}
    \caption{Response time under concurrent load conditions. }
    \label{fig:concurrency-response}
\end{figure}

Interestingly, despite the throughput advantage of monolithic architecture, response time measurements 
(Figure \ref{fig:concurrency-response}) show no significant difference between the two approaches. 
Both architectures start at approximately 1,790 milliseconds with 100 concurrent requests and degrade 
to around 15,000 milliseconds at 1,000 requests. This suggests that while monolithic architecture 
can process more total requests per second, individual request latency remains comparable — a finding 
with important implications for perceived user experience.



\subsubsection{Implications for LMS Architecture Selection}

The empirical evidence from Al-Debagy and Martinek's comparative study reveals nuanced performance 
trade-offs rather than a clear winner. For LMS deployments:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-to-medium scale systems} (fewer than 1,000 concurrent users) may benefit from 
    monolithic architecture's throughput advantages, particularly during peak concurrent load events 
    such as assignment submissions or exam periods.
    
    \item \textbf{Large-scale systems} experiencing graduated load increases show negligible performance 
    differences, suggesting architectural choice should be driven by other criteria (maintainability, 
    scalability, deployment flexibility) rather than raw performance.
    
    \item \textbf{Response time sensitivity} — critical for user satisfaction — shows no architectural 
    dependency, indicating that perceived performance will be comparable regardless of architecture 
    choice, assuming equivalent infrastructure resources.
    
    \item \textbf{The 'Performance-to-Complexity' Ratio:} The marginal 6\% performance gain of the Monolith must 
    be weighed against the Developmental Agility of Microservice. For an institution 
    with a stable, predictable student base, the Monolith offers a superior performance-to-cost 
    ratio. However, for a global E-learning provider, the ability to independently scale 
    the \textit{Assessment Service} during exam peaks often outweighs the minor throughput penalty recorded in Al-Debagy’s research.

\item \textbf{Saturation Behavior:} The study's observation that both architectures saturate beyond 4,000 threads suggests that Hardware and Resource Allocation remain the ultimate limiters. Architectural choice determines how gracefully the system fails (Resilience) rather than if it fails under extreme overloads.

\end{itemize}

These findings inform the experimental design of this project's change scenarios, where performance 
will be evaluated not as an isolated criterion but in conjunction with development effort, code 
complexity, and architectural evolution costs. The evidence suggests that for typical LMS workloads, 
architectural performance differences are marginal, making the evaluation of maintainability and 
evolvability criteria  potentially more decisive factors in architecture selection.


\subsection{CPU Usage}

Schlögl et al. (2024) in their empirical study \cite{Schloegl2024} define \textit{Resource Efficiency} as the system's ability to minimize hardware overhead while fulfilling functional requirements. The authors emphasize that CPU utilization is a critical trade-off factor when migrating from Monolithic to Microservice. 

A fundamental contribution of the study by Bernsteiner et al. (2024) is the distinction between Service-level and VM-level CPU monitoring. In a Monolithic architecture, the CPU usage is almost entirely dedicated to the application logic. However, in Microservice, the CPU is 'taxed' by the infrastructure. This tax includes the overhead of the Docker daemon, the container's isolated networking stack, and the resource-intensive process of Service Discovery and Load Balancing performed by the API Gateway. Consequently, the total VM-level CPU usage often presents a 'Shifted Baseline,' where significant energy and processing power are consumed even in an idle state.

To provide a comprehensive evaluation, the authors conducted experiments across three distinct test cases, each representing a different operational scenario.
In these test cases, the authors use two types of charts for Microservice architecture to provide a comprehensive view: Service-level 
CPU represents business logic processing performance and VM-level CPU represents the actual total resource cost 
including container orchestration. The difference between these two charts illustrates the typical Overhead 
costs of Microservice, which if only observed at the application layer will be easily overlooked.

\subsubsection{Test Case 1: Simple Database Query}
In this scenario, the system performs a basic retrieval of data (e.g., fetching a user profile). The authors noted that 
the Microservice architecture already exhibited a higher CPU baseline compared to the Monolith.
The actual testing results show even for simple tasks, the overhead of the API Gateway and the internal 
networking stack in Microservice leads to measurable CPU spikes that are non-existent in 
the direct function calls of a Monolith.

The testing results for simple queries highlight the 'Proxy Latency' effect. While the Monolith executes a database call through a local connection pool, the Microservice request must be intercepted by the Nginx/API Gateway, routed through a virtual bridge network, and then handled by the service. Each 'hop' requires CPU cycles for packet inspection and routing. Schlögl et al. observed that this results in a 'Fragmentation of Resources': instead of one concentrated CPU spike, multiple containers exhibit smaller, simultaneous spikes, which collectively exceed the Monolith's energy footprint.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{images/papers/cpu-usage-test-case-1.png}
    \label{fig:cpu-tc1}
\end{figure}

While the Monolith shows higher Peak CPU due to the concentration of multi-core processing on 
a single process, Microservice shows higher overall resource consumption. This is 
demonstrated by the baseline load level of the 5 VM-Instanz always remaining at 10-15\% even before testing, 
and the appearance of overhead costs at API Gateway when the load starts. The Monolith exhibits a burst-and-idle resource 
consumption pattern: CPU utilization spikes sharply during request processing and returns to near-zero during 
inter-request intervals. The Microservices architecture, by contrast, sustains a continuous baseline resource 
draw to maintain network infrastructure — connection pools, health-check polling, and message broker 
scheduling — regardless of application-level request volume. This structural difference means that Microservices incur a non-zero minimum resource cost that persists even under zero user load, a characteristic that directly affects the cost-of-readiness analysis presented in Section 6.4.

This test case involves logic-heavy tasks, such as complex calculations or data transformation before returning the result.
The performance gap between the two architectures widened significantly. 
 The actual testing results show The CPU in Microservice is doubly burdened: first by the 
 intensive business logic and second by the \textit{Serialization/Deserialization} (JSON parsing) required to 
 move large data objects between services. The Monolith, however, processes this data within a single 
 memory space, maintaining higher efficiency.

The 'Double Burden' in Test Case 2 is primarily driven by Data Marshalling. 
 In Microservice, complex data objects must be serialized into JSON at the source 
 service and deserialized at the receiving service. Schlögl's data reveals that 
 for logic-heavy tasks, up to 20-30\% of the total CPU time in Microservice is spent on 
 string parsing and memory allocation for these temporary JSON objects, rather than the 
 core business logic. The Monolithic architecture avoids this entirely by passing object 
 references in shared memory, allowing it to maintain a lower and more stable temperature and CPU profile during heavy processing


\subsubsection{Test Case 3: Distributed Transactions / Multi-service Orchestration}
This scenario simulates a workflow that requires coordination between multiple services (e.g., an enrollment process involving Auth, Course, and Payment services).
This case showed the highest degree of CPU saturation for the Microservice version.
The actual testing results show The "network hop" effect becomes prominent. Each additional service involved in the transaction adds cumulative CPU cycles for managing multiple connection pools and security handshakes, leading to an exponential increase in total CPU load under high concurrency.

In scenario 3, the authors identify the 'Cascading CPU Load' phenomenon. Each microservice involved in a distributed transaction (Auth, Enrollment, Assessment) maintains its own independent runtime and garbage collection (GC) cycles. Under high load, these GC events can overlap, creating massive, aggregate CPU 'jitter.' Furthermore, the persistent HTTP/1.1 or gRPC connections required for orchestration consume 'Keep-alive' CPU cycles. This explains why the Microservice version reached its Saturation Point significantly earlier than the Monolith, despite having access to the same underlying VM resources.

\subsection{Fault Tolerance}


The concept of \textit{Fault Tolerance} refers to the ability of a system to maintain operation or undergo controlled 
degradation (graceful degradation) when a component fails. In the research conducted by Nagaraj Parvatha (2020), a 
theoretical framework was proposed alongside a set of techniques aimed at improving fault tolerance and recovery in 
Microservice systems \cite{IJNRD2023}. Within this context, \textit{availability} is identified as the most 
fundamental metric for evaluating system resilience. Based on the theoretical foundation presented in 
the book \textit{Reliability and Availability of Cloud Computing} by Eric Bauer and Randee Adams \cite{bauer2012cloud}, availability is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\text{Availability} = \frac{MTBF}{MTBF + MTTR} \times 100\%
\label{eq:availability}
\end{equation}

where:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)} represents the average time between system failures, reflecting system reliability. 
    MTBF is calculated as the ratio of the total stable operating time to the total number of error occurrences
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
MTBF = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( t^{\text{start}}_i - t^{\text{recovery}}_{i-1} \right)}{n}
\label{eq:mtbf}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery)} denotes the average time required to restore the system after a failure. 
    MTTR is calculated as the ratio of the total fault recovery time to the total number of fault occurrences
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
MTTR = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} \left( t^{\text{recovery}}_i - t^{\text{failure}}_i \right)}{n}
\label{eq:mttr}
\end{equation}

Nagaraj Parvatha highlighted that Microservice architecture can reduce MTTR by up to 66\% (from an average of 58 minutes to 19 minutes). This improvement is primarily achieved through \textit{service isolation}, which prevents cascading failures. When a specific service (e.g., \texttt{grading-service}) fails, it does not affect the entire system as in a monolithic architecture, thereby significantly reducing the time required for fault detection and recovery.

To validate this theoretical claim, the system will simulate a failure scenario by intentionally interrupting the grading service container. The recovery time will be measured from the moment the service becomes unavailable until it is either automatically restored or successfully restarted (i.e., when the system reaches the \texttt{Health check: Pass} state).

\subsection{Cost Efficiency}

This criterion evaluates the economic and operational viability of the architecture. According to the empirical 
study by Yogant Faye (2025) \cite{Yogant_Faye}, which synthesized data from 23 architectural 
migrations, "Cost" in modern systems is a multi-dimensional construct comprising Infrastructure
Expenditure, Operational Overhead (DevOps), and the Complexity Tax associated with system maintenance.

Faye’s findings indicate a stark contrast in cost profiles: while transitioning to Microservice 
leads to an average infrastructure cost increase of 47\%, the operational "Complexity Tax" — specifically
in DevOps tooling and observability — surges by 150\% to 275\%. To determine whether this 
increased expenditure translates into better performance, we utilize the Resource Utilization Efficiency (RUE) metric:

\begin{equation}
RUE = \frac{\text{Number of processing requests}}{\text{Provisioned Resources (CPU/RAM usage)}}
\label{eq:rue}
\end{equation}

where \textit{Workload} represents the number of processed 
requests, and \textit{Provisioned Resources} refers to the 
allocated computational resources (e.g., CPU and RAM).

\subsubsection{The Baseline vs. Elasticity Trade-off}

The research by Faye introduces the Architecture Selection Index (ASI), which suggests that the Cost Efficiency of Microservice only surpasses that of a Monolith when certain operational thresholds are met.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The Infrastructure Tax (Monolithic Advantage):} At low-to-moderate scales, the Monolithic architecture exhibits a superior RUE because it avoids the "Infrastructure Tax"—the baseline resource consumption required for inter-service communication (API Gateways, Service Mesh) and independent runtimes. Faye's meta-analysis confirms that for teams smaller than 25 developers, the Monolith remains the most cost-effective choice.

\item \textbf{Scaling Granularity (Microservice Advantage):} The RUE of Microservice becomes dominant when the system faces high \textbf{Traffic Variability} (exceeding a 2:1 ratio). In an LMS context, if the independent scaling needs of a specific module (e.g., \texttt{grading-service}) exceed 40\% of the total service load, Microservice prevent "Resource Over-provisioning." Unlike a Monolith, which requires replicating the entire application stack to handle a spike in one module, Microservice allow for targeted resource allocation, thereby optimizing the RUE during peak periods.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Operational Cost and Long-term ROI}

Faye’s survey of 67 software architects reveals that while Microservice have a higher "Cost of Entry," they achieve a 
higher Maintainability Score (4.2/5 vs. 2.4/5). From a Cost Efficiency perspective, this reduces 
the "Mean Time To Repair" (MTTR) and minimizes the cost of technical debt.

The study concludes that Microservice act as a "Scalability Insurance." The higher initial investment and the 47\% increase in infrastructure 
costs are justified only when the system demands independent evolution of services. For an LMS, this means the RUE is 
maximized not by minimizing the cloud bill, but by ensuring that resource consumption scales linearly with the pedagogical 
demand of specific functional domains.

Both implementations utilize NestJS to enforce modular boundaries and clear separation of concerns.

\subsection{PostgreSQL}

PostgreSQL serves as the relational database across both architectures, but database 
organization differs meaningfully. In the Monolithic architecture, all entities (Students, Courses, Enrollments, Grades, 
etc.) reside in a single shared schema, creating high database-level coupling. All 
application modules access the same tables directly.

In the Microservice architecture, each service maintains its own schema (database 
per service pattern) within the same PostgreSQL instance. Cross-service data queries 
are resolved through API calls rather than shared database access, providing stronger 
data isolation but requiring additional coordination mechanisms (API composition, 
event propagation via RabbitMQ) for consistency.

This difference is a key evaluation point for Change Scenarios, where modifications 
to grading logic (Scenario 1) and progress tracking (Scenario 2) reveal how each 
architecture accommodates evolving data requirements.

\subsection{Communication Paradigms: Synchronous vs. Asynchronous}
The effectiveness of a Microservice-based LMS is largely determined by its inter-service communication strategy. Theoretical literature identifies two primary patterns:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Synchronous Request-Response:} REST or gRPC, where a service waits for a direct response. While easier to implement, this can lead to "Cascading Failures" if one service in the chain slows down.
\item \textbf{Asynchronous Messaging:} Leveraging message brokers such as \textbf{RabbitMQ}. This pattern decouples services, allowing them to process tasks at their own pace. For an LMS, this is vital for non-blocking operations such as sending notification emails or generating background analytics reports.
\end{itemize}
This study integrates both patterns to evaluate how they contribute to the system's overall \textit{Fault Tolerance} and \textit{Resource Utilization Efficiency (RUE)}, moving beyond simple process separation to true operational decoupling.

\subsection{Docker and Containerization}
Docker is used to containerize all architectural implementations, ensuring consistent deployment environments and enabling fair performance comparisons. Each architecture is packaged as a Docker Compose configuration specifying the required services, database instances, and network topology.

For the Monolithic architecture, a single application container is defined alongside a database container.
For the Microservice architecture, individual containers are defined per service, along with a shared API Gateway 
container and service-specific database containers. This configuration difference itself constitutes a measurable
dimension of operational complexity in the comparative evaluation.

Furthermore, the use of containerization transcends simple packaging; it represents the shift towards \textbf{Cloud-Native} engineering. By defining the infrastructure via Docker Compose, the research ensures "Environmental Parity," eliminating the "it works on my machine" phenomenon. This allows for a rigorous "ceteris paribus" comparison, where the only variable is the architectural topology. The study also draws on the theory of \textbf{Micro-segmentation}, where each container’s resource limits (CPU and Memory) are strictly defined, allowing for an empirical analysis of how each architecture handles "Resource Contention" under the 2,000 VU stress test scenarios defined in later chapters
